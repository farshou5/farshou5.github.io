01

The verdict, in one paragraph

Keep your story. Change the order. Change the title.

All three of us — separately, and then against each other — landed in the same place: the problem was never that the story is weak. The problem is that the best thing in it arrives at minute three. A machine offering to bring your father back, and you saying no, is not the ending of your film. It is your film. So we moved the death to the first frame, made the lenses an inheritance instead of a lesson, and fused your 24-hour good-deed rule to it: the lenses keep your father alive only while you keep earning him. You become the man he wanted — for the wrong reason. Then you do one good thing with the lenses off, where nothing can count it, and let him go.

That last move is the title. A machine can verify behaviour. It cannot verify goodness.

What we did NOT do, and why

Codex's first answer was to throw the film away and make a different one — a Beirut blackout, a hand-cranked lamp, your father's shadow looping on a wall. It was beautiful, and it lost. Reason: a new concept restarts the two stages most likely to fail (concept and script) with 19 days left, and it throws away your only unfair advantage — that this story is yours. Kimi put it flatly: His question assumes the story might be the problem. It isn't. The form is. Codex dropped its own film in the next round and joined the restructure.

So: no new film. Your film, in the right order, at a third of the runtime, with a tenth of the lip-sync risk.

02

Why polishing the 4:55 rebuild was not enough

Four things came out of the re-analysis that the last version could not survive.

1 · The dialogue arithmetic

Kimi counted the script I sent you last week: 58 numbered lines, 46 of them spoken by humans on camera. Every single one is a lip-sync gamble at 3–5 rejected takes each, on a browser plan that generates one job at a time, judged by a cinematographer who has spent forty years watching mouths. That plan spends your 19 days fighting the one thing AI does worst. UNCOUNTED has three shots where a visible mouth has to match a spoken line.

2 · It looks like television

I went back through the actual images this time, not just the transcript. The 7:41 cut is flat, eye-level, evenly lit, mostly two men on a couch in a wide. Visual craft is one of the four official judging criteria, and the jury includes the cinematographer of Ford v Ferrari and the director of Dark City. The two most cinematic images in your whole film are the motorcycle POV with the HUD and the bouquet on the grave — and both survive into the new cut.

3 · Screening kills you before the good part

Three stages: Screening → Shortlist → Jury. Most entries die at Screening, and a previous Higgsfield contest drew 8,752 of them. The old structure spends its first three minutes explaining how a device works before revealing what the film is about. Codex: Saving the father simulation for minute three means hiding the film's strongest dramatic question until after many screeners will have rejected it.

4 · The premise was the most-made AI short of the last three years

"AI shows a man the consequences of his choices" is Black Mirror-adjacent and everywhere. Fair warning in the other direction, from both of them: "grieving man talks to an AI ghost" is even more crowded. What is not crowded — what none of us could find anywhere — is the compound: the ghost is hostage to your virtue, and the act that frees you is the one the machine cannot see. That is the originality score, and it is defensible in one sentence.

03

The film

Logline

After his father dies, Moe finds that the AI lenses his father built will keep a reconstruction of him alive — but only while they verify one good deed every twenty-four hours. To prove he has actually changed, he performs the first deed the machine cannot witness, and lets his father go.

The five rules the whole film obeys

  • Present day is seen through the lenses. POV, HUD, machine perspective.
  • The past is objective, warm, and machine-free. No HUD, no machine text, anywhere in the flashback. That is how you feel the difference between having him and rendering him.
  • When he takes the lenses out, the camera steps outside him for the first time — and the HUD never comes back. Form, character and climax become the same decision.
  • The machine writes; it never speaks. Silent on-screen text is its voice for the entire film. The first time you hear it out loud is the case in his hand at the grave, telling him his father is ending — and that is why it lands.
  • The simulated father is stable, warm and still — never glitchy. He is marked as a reconstruction by framing, by a locked composition, and by a percentage that quietly decays: 91 → 89 → 88.

On that last rule — I was overruled, and they were right

My idea was to let the father glitch, so that every AI artifact would read as intentional. Codex called it mostly rationalisation: a cinematographer recognises intention through consistency, and a film full of broken faces reads as failed generation with an excuse stapled on. So the artifacts are out and the decay meter stays — one number, ticking down, doing the same job honestly.

The seven generations — the film in 30-second blocks

Seedance generates 30 seconds at a time, so this is the film as it will actually be built: seven blocks, in order, 7 × 30 = 3:30. Each block is one row of the Cinema Studio timeline. Inside a block, every JOB is a separate generation — never ask one job to change location or camera side mid-clip.

The five rules of generation (Codex + Kimi audited this plan on 17 Aug; these came out of it)

  • Machine text is never generated pixels. Every HUD line, timer and percentage is an in-tool overlay on a clean plate. Generated text garbles and burns retries; overlays are deterministic and always legible. Both auditors called this the plan's #1 risk.
  • One location, one camera side, per job. A "single shot" that is secretly a montage is the schedule killer: one failed hand forces a full regeneration.
  • Line numbers below are the same numbers as the script above. "6.03" here is 6.03 there.
  • Three HERO shots (1.09, 4.03, 6.03) are the only mouth-matches-voice gambles — eight to ten takes each, generated FIRST (they decide GO/NO-GO). Everything else is voice-over, backs, helmets or silent text — two or three takes.
  • Paste the identity card into every prompt. One line per character: Soul ID + wardrobe + stubble state. Unshaven vigil-Moe is the same Soul with a stubble modifier, never a new identity. The original film's drift (four wardrobes on one man) is the proof this matters.
